Week 5: Communication Protocols in Embedded Systems

1. Why Do Embedded Systems Need Communication Protocols?

Imagine a modern car or a smart home: you have dozens of sensors measuring temperature, wheel speed, and battery voltage, along with multiple microcontrollers controlling motors, displays, and actuators.

If every sensor required its own dedicated bundle of analog wires directly connected to the central CPU, the wiring harness would weigh hundreds of kilograms and cost thousands of dollars.

 WITHOUT A COMMUNICATION PROTOCOL (Chaotic & Expensive):
 [Sensor 1] ======= 8 Dedicated Wires =======> [ Microcontroller ]
 [Sensor 2] ======= 8 Dedicated Wires =======> [ Microcontroller ]
 [Display ] ======= 16 Dedicated Wires ======> [ Microcontroller ]
 * Bulky wiring, pin exhaustion, high cost, and zero interoperability!

 WITH A STANDARDIZED PROTOCOL (Organized & Efficient):
 [Sensor 1] --+
 [Sensor 2] --+=== Shared 2-Wire Serial Bus (e.g., I2C / CAN) ===> [ Microcontroller ]
 [Display ] --+
 * Only 2 to 4 shared wires, low cost, scalable, and standardized!

A Communication Protocol is simply an agreed-upon set of rules that defines: 1. Physical Signaling: Voltage levels, timing intervals, and clock frequencies. 2. Data Framing: How bits are grouped into packets, bytes, start bits, and stop bits. 3. Addressing: How a master processor identifies which specific sensor it wants to talk to on a shared bus. 4. Error Handling: How the receiver detects if electrical noise corrupted a transmitted bit (e.g., Parity, Checksum, CRC).

2. Serial vs. Parallel Communication

In the early days of computing, parallel communication (sending 8, 16, or 32 bits simultaneously over 8, 16, or 32 physical wires) was faster because clock speeds were low. However, in modern embedded systems, serial communication (sending data bit-by-bit over a single wire) has almost completely replaced parallel buses.

 PARALLEL BUS (8 Bits Sent Simultaneously)
 Sender [D0] -----------------------------> Receiver [D0]
        [D1] ----------------------------->          [D1]
        [D2] ----------------------------->          [D2]
        ...
        [CLK] ---------------------------->          [CLK]
 * Problem: Clock Skew! Bits arrive at slightly different nanoseconds over different trace lengths.

 SERIAL BUS (1 Bit Sent at a Time in Rapid Succession)
 Sender [TX] ---- Bit 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0 ----> Receiver [RX]
 * Solved: Zero trace-to-trace skew, simple 1-wire trace, works reliably over long distances!

Why Serial Communication Won:

  • No Clock Skew: In parallel buses, slight differences in PCB copper trace lengths cause bits to arrive at slightly different times (clock skew). At high speeds, this scrambles data. Serial sends one bit at a time, eliminating skew.
  • Fewer Microcontroller Pins: Serial protocols only require 1 to 4 pins, allowing microcontrollers to come in tiny, inexpensive chip packages (QFN, TSSOP).
  • Reduced Cross-Talk & Noise: Routing 16 parallel high-speed traces close together causes electromagnetic interference (cross-talk). Serial routing is clean and compact.
  • Longer Distance: Serial signals can travel meters or even kilometers (e.g., RS-485, CAN bus), whereas parallel buses are restricted to a few centimeters.

3. The Big Three Microcontroller Serial Protocols

Microcontrollers typically feature hardware peripheral engines for three fundamental serial standards: UART, SPI, and \(I^2C\).

 +-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | Feature             | UART                       | SPI                  | I2C           |
 +---------------------+----------------------------+----------------------+---------------+
 | Type                | Asynchronous (No clock)    | Synchronous (SCK)    | Synchronous   |
 | Wires (Excl. GND)   | 2 (TX, RX)                 | 4 (MOSI, MISO,SCK,CS)| 2 (SDA, SCL)  |

 +-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

3.1 UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter)

UART is an asynchronous protocol. “Asynchronous” means there is no shared clock wire between the sender and the receiver.

Instead, both devices are pre-configured to run at the exact same transmission speed, known as the Baud Rate (e.g., 9600, 115200 bits per second).

 UART WIRING (Cross-over connection):
 MCU 1 [TX Pin] ------------------------------> MCU 2 [RX Pin]
 MCU 1 [RX Pin] <------------------------------ MCU 2 [TX Pin]
 MCU 1 [GND   ] ------------------------------- MCU 2 [GND   ]

The UART Data Frame

When no data is being sent, the line idles at a steady logic HIGH (\(V_{CC}\)). To transmit a byte, the UART hardware wraps the 8 data bits in a standardized frame:

 Idle (1) | Start (0) | D0 | D1 | D2 | D3 | D4 | D5 | D6 | D7 | Parity | Stop (1) | Idle (1)
 ---------+           +----+----+----+----+----+----+----+----+--------+----------+---------
          |___________|                                                |
            1 Bit-Time             8 Data Bits (LSB First)                1-2 Bits
  1. Start Bit (Always 0): The sender pulls the line LOW for 1 bit-time. This falling edge wakes up the receiver and starts its internal sampling timer.
  2. Data Payload (5 to 9 bits, typically 8 bits): The byte is sent Least Significant Bit (LSB) first.
  3. Optional Parity Bit: Adds basic error detection (Even or Odd parity).
  4. Stop Bit(s) (Always 1): The line returns HIGH for 1 or 2 bit-times, allowing the receiver to shift the byte into its buffer and prepare for the next frame.

3.2 SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface)

SPI is a synchronous, full-duplex bus designed by Motorola. Because it includes an explicit clock line (SCK) driven by the Master, it can achieve speeds exceeding \(50\text{ MHz}\).

  • MOSI (Master Out Slave In): Data sent from the Master to the Slave.
  • MISO (Master In Slave Out): Data sent from the Slave back to the Master.
  • SCK (Serial Clock): Clock pulses generated exclusively by the Master to time bit transfers.
  • CS / SS (Chip Select / Slave Select): Active-low pin used to wake up and select a specific slave.

3.3 \(I^2C\) (Inter-Integrated Circuit)

\(I^2C\) is a synchronous, half-duplex, multi-device bus that requires only two wires, regardless of how many devices are attached: - SDA (Serial Data): Bi-directional data line. - SCL (Serial Clock): Synchronizing clock line driven by the Master.

Open-Drain Architecture & Pull-Up Resistors

\(I^2C\) pins use open-drain output stages. They can pull the line LOW to \(0\text{V}\), but cannot actively drive it HIGH. External pull-up resistors (\(R_p\), typically \(4.7\text{ k}\Omega\)) pull the bus voltage back up to \(3.3\text{V}\) when all devices release the line.

4. Networking Concepts & Industrial Fieldbus Protocols

In harsh factory environments or automobiles, consumer protocols like \(I^2C\) fail due to electrical motor noise. Fieldbus protocols provide robust, noise-immune communication: - CAN Bus (Controller Area Network): Used across automotive ECUs with balanced differential signaling (CAN_H and CAN_L). - RS-485 / Modbus: Industrial balanced serial standard for distances up to 1200 meters. - LIN Bus: Single-wire cost-effective automotive bus for sub-systems like power mirrors and window switches. - Wireless Protocols (BLE, Zigbee, LoRa): For cable-free remote IoT and sensor node networking.

5. Mini-Project 1: Two-Way UART Command & Echo Parser 📡💬

🎯 Objective

In field automation and diagnostic consoles, microcontrollers frequently communicate with a host computer or test harness via a serial terminal. In this project, you will build an interactive ASCII Command-Line Interface (CLI) over UART at 115200 baud running on the STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 that parses incoming text commands, toggles the onboard LED, and replies with structured acknowledgement frames.

🛠️ Hardware Setup (Wokwi)

The circuit connects the following to an STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 board: * Onboard User LED: Connected to pin PA5 (D13) to visualize command states. * UART USB VCP: Direct hardware serial link over ST-LINK Virtual COM Port. * (Optional breadboard LED: Anode to D13 via 220Ω resistor, Cathode to GND).

📝 Problem Statement & Logic Flow

  1. Initialization (setup):
    • Start UART Serial communication at 115200 baud rate.
    • Configure onboard LED pin (D13 / PA5) as OUTPUT and set initial state to LOW.
    • Print a startup welcome banner listing all supported CLI commands.
  2. Continuous Loop (loop):
    • Poll Receive Buffer: Check if characters are available with Serial.available().
    • Extract Command Line: Read incoming characters until newline (\n) and trim whitespace.
    • Parse Commands (Case-Insensitive):
      • "LED ON" \(\rightarrow\) Drive D13 HIGH and reply "[ACK] LED is now ON".
      • "LED OFF" \(\rightarrow\) Drive D13 LOW and reply "[ACK] LED is now OFF".
      • "LED TOGGLE" \(\rightarrow\) Invert current LED state and report new state.
      • "PING" \(\rightarrow\) Reply "[ACK] PONG!".
      • "STATUS" \(\rightarrow\) Report current LED status and uptime in seconds (millis() / 1000).
    • Defensive Fallback: If command is unrecognized, report "[ERR] Unknown command: <cmd>".

// ============================================================================
// Mini-Project 1: Two-Way UART Command & Echo Parser (Nucleo-C031C6)
// ============================================================================

#define LED_PIN LED_BUILTIN // PA5 / D13

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);

  Serial.println("======================================");
  Serial.println("  STM32 UART Command Parser Ready     ");
  Serial.println("  Commands: 'LED ON', 'LED OFF', 'PING'");
  Serial.println("======================================");
}

void loop() {
  if (Serial.available() > 0) {
    String cmd = Serial.readStringUntil('\n');
    cmd.trim(); // Strip newline and carriage returns

    if (cmd.equalsIgnoreCase("LED ON")) {
      digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);
      Serial.println("[ACK] LED is now ON");
    } else if (cmd.equalsIgnoreCase("LED OFF")) {
      digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);
      Serial.println("[ACK] LED is now OFF");
    } else if (cmd.equalsIgnoreCase("LED TOGGLE")) {
      digitalWrite(LED_PIN, !digitalRead(LED_PIN));
      Serial.print("[ACK] LED toggled to: ");
      Serial.println(digitalRead(LED_PIN) ? "ON" : "OFF");
    } else if (cmd.equalsIgnoreCase("PING")) {
      Serial.println("[ACK] PONG!");
    } else if (cmd.equalsIgnoreCase("STATUS")) {
      Serial.print("[STATUS] LED: ");
      Serial.print(digitalRead(LED_PIN) ? "ON" : "OFF");
      Serial.print(" | Uptime: ");
      Serial.print(millis() / 1000);
      Serial.println("s");
    } else if (cmd.length() > 0) {
      Serial.print("[ERR] Unknown command: ");
      Serial.println(cmd);
    }
  }
}

// Mini-Project 1: Bare-Metal STM32 UART Driver (Direct Register Access)
// Hardware: STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 (USART2 on PA2/PA3, LED on PA5)
// ============================================================================
#include <stdint.h>

#define RCC_BASE        (0x40021000UL)
#define RCC_IOPENR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(RCC_BASE + 0x34UL))
#define RCC_APBENR1     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(RCC_BASE + 0x3CUL))

#define GPIOA_BASE      (0x50000000UL)
#define GPIOA_MODER     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOA_BASE + 0x00UL))
#define GPIOA_AFRL      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOA_BASE + 0x20UL))
#define GPIOA_BSRR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOA_BASE + 0x18UL))

#define USART2_BASE     (0x40004400UL)
#define USART2_CR1      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(USART2_BASE + 0x00UL))
#define USART2_BRR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(USART2_BASE + 0x0CUL))
#define USART2_ISR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(USART2_BASE + 0x1CUL))
#define USART2_TDR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(USART2_BASE + 0x28UL))
#define USART2_RDR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(USART2_BASE + 0x24UL))

void uart_init(uint32_t pclk, uint32_t baud) {
    RCC_IOPENR  |= (1UL << 0);        // Enable GPIOA clock
    RCC_APBENR1 |= (1UL << 17);       // Enable USART2 clock

    // Configure PA2 as Alternate Function (AF1 for USART2_TX)
    GPIOA_MODER &= ~(3UL << (2 * 2));
    GPIOA_MODER |=  (2UL << (2 * 2));
    GPIOA_AFRL  |=  (1UL << (2 * 4));

    // Configure PA5 as Output (User LED)
    GPIOA_MODER &= ~(3UL << (5 * 2));
    GPIOA_MODER |=  (1UL << (5 * 2));

    // Set Baud Rate (pclk / baud)
    USART2_BRR = pclk / baud;
    USART2_CR1 = (1UL << 3) | (1UL << 2) | (1UL << 0); // TE, RE, UE
}

void uart_send_char(char c) {
    while (!(USART2_ISR & (1UL << 7))); // Wait for TXE (Transmit Empty)
    USART2_TDR = (uint8_t)c;
}

void uart_send_str(const char *s) {
    while (*s) uart_send_char(*s++);
}

6. Mini-Project 2: EV Battery Thermal & Voltage Supervisor 🔋⚡

🎯 Objective

In Electric Vehicle (EV) battery management systems, thermal runaway can cause catastrophic fires. In this mini-project, you will build an autonomous EV Battery Thermal Transducer & Alarm Supervisor on the STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 that samples an analog temperature transducer and triggers safety indicators and alarms.

🛠️ Hardware Setup (Wokwi)

The circuit connects the following components to an STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 board: * 1x Potentiometer (wokwi-potentiometer): Connected to A0 (PA0) to simulate the analog temperature transducer (\(0.0^\circ\text{C}\) to \(100.0^\circ\text{C}\)). * 1x Green Status LED: Connected to D2 (PA10) via \(220\,\Omega\) resistor (Safe State Indicator). * 1x Red Hazard LED: Connected to D3 (PB3) via \(220\,\Omega\) resistor (Overheat Hazard Indicator). * 1x Warning Buzzer (wokwi-buzzer): Connected to D5 (PB4) for audible emergency alerting.

Pin Wiring Table

Component Pin / Terminal on Component STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 Pin Electrical Notes
Potentiometer GND / VCC GND / 3.3V Analog Reference Power Rails
Potentiometer SIG (Wiper) A0 (PA0) Analog Thermal Transducer (\(0.0^\circ\text{C}-100.0^\circ\text{C}\))
Safe LED (Green) Anode (via 220Ω) D2 (PA10) Normal thermal state indicator
Hazard LED (Red) Anode (via 220Ω) D3 (PB3) Thermal runaway hazard indicator
Warning Buzzer Positive (\(+\)) D5 (PB4) Audible alarm actuator
Common Ground Cathodes / GND GND Ground Reference

📝 Problem Statement & Functional Requirements

  1. Transducer Acquisition:
    • Sample the analog temperature transducer on pin A0 continuously.
    • Map the 10-bit ADC reading (\(0 - 1023\)) to temperature: \(\text{Temp}(^\circ\text{C}) = \frac{\text{raw} \times 100.0}{1023.0}\).
  2. Thermal State Decision Logic:
    • Safe State (< 45.0°C): Green LED on D2 is ON, Red LED on D3 is OFF, and Buzzer on D5 is OFF.
    • Hazard State (>= 45.0°C): Green LED is OFF, Red LED is ON, Buzzer pulses at 2 Hz (250 ms ON, 250 ms OFF), and send "[ALERT] OVERHEAT HAZARD: <temp> C" over UART at 115200 baud.
  3. Non-Blocking Constraint: The buzzer pulse is driven using millis() timing comparisons.

// ============================================================================
// Mini-Project 2: EV Battery Thermal & Voltage Supervisor
// Target Board: STM32 Nucleo-C031C6
// ============================================================================

#define SENSOR_PIN    A0 // Temperature Transducer (0 - 100 C)
#define LED_GREEN_PIN 2  // D2 Safe State
#define LED_RED_PIN   3  // D3 Hazard State
#define BUZZER_PIN    5  // D5 Alarm Buzzer

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_GREEN_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(LED_RED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(BUZZER_PIN, OUTPUT);
  
  digitalWrite(LED_GREEN_PIN, HIGH);
  digitalWrite(LED_RED_PIN, LOW);
  digitalWrite(BUZZER_PIN, LOW);
}

void loop() {
  int raw = analogRead(SENSOR_PIN);
  float temp = (raw * 100.0f) / 1023.0f; // 0.0 to 100.0 °C

  static unsigned long lastToggle = 0;
  static bool buzzerState = false;

  if (temp >= 45.0f) { // Overheat hazard
    digitalWrite(LED_GREEN_PIN, LOW);
    digitalWrite(LED_RED_PIN, HIGH);
    
    // 2 Hz non-blocking toggle (250 ms ON, 250 ms OFF)
    if (millis() - lastToggle >= 250) {
      lastToggle = millis();
      buzzerState = !buzzerState;
      digitalWrite(BUZZER_PIN, buzzerState ? HIGH : LOW);
    }
    Serial.print("[ALERT] OVERHEAT HAZARD: ");
  } else { // Safe state
    digitalWrite(LED_GREEN_PIN, HIGH);
    digitalWrite(LED_RED_PIN, LOW);
    digitalWrite(BUZZER_PIN, LOW);
    Serial.print("[STATUS] Battery Temp Safe: ");
  }

  Serial.print(temp, 1);
  Serial.println(" C");
  delay(250);
}

// Mini-Project 2: Bare-Metal EV Battery Thermal & Voltage Supervisor
// Target: STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 (ARM Cortex-M0+)
// ============================================================================
#include <stdint.h>
#include <stdbool.h>

// 1. Bus Clock Registers
#define RCC_BASE        (0x40021000UL)
#define RCC_IOPENR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(RCC_BASE + 0x34UL))
#define RCC_APBENR2     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(RCC_BASE + 0x40UL))

// 2. GPIO Registers (GPIOA & GPIOB)
#define GPIOA_BASE      (0x50000000UL)
#define GPIOA_MODER     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOA_BASE + 0x00UL))
#define GPIOA_BSRR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOA_BASE + 0x18UL))

#define GPIOB_BASE      (0x50000400UL)
#define GPIOB_MODER     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOB_BASE + 0x00UL))
#define GPIOB_BSRR      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(GPIOB_BASE + 0x18UL))

// 3. ADC1 Registers
#define ADC1_BASE       (0x40012400UL)
#define ADC1_ISR        (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC1_BASE + 0x00UL))
#define ADC1_CR         (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC1_BASE + 0x08UL))
#define ADC1_CHSELR     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC1_BASE + 0x28UL))
#define ADC1_DR         (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC1_BASE + 0x40UL))

void hardware_init(void) {
    // Enable GPIOA, GPIOB, and ADC1 peripheral bus clocks
    RCC_IOPENR |= (1UL << 0) | (1UL << 1);
    RCC_APBENR2 |= (1UL << 20); // ADC1EN

    // PA0 (A0) as Analog Mode ('11')
    GPIOA_MODER |= (3UL << (0 * 2));

    // PA10 (D2 Green LED) as General Purpose Output ('01')
    GPIOA_MODER &= ~(3UL << (10 * 2));
    GPIOA_MODER |=  (1UL << (10 * 2));

    // PB3 (D3 Red LED) as General Purpose Output ('01')
    GPIOB_MODER &= ~(3UL << (3 * 2));
    GPIOB_MODER |=  (1UL << (3 * 2));

    // PB4 (D5 Buzzer) as General Purpose Output ('01')
    GPIOB_MODER &= ~(3UL << (4 * 2));
    GPIOB_MODER |=  (1UL << (4 * 2));

    // Configure ADC1: Channel 0 (PA0)
    ADC1_CHSELR = (1UL << 0);
    ADC1_CR |= (1UL << 0); // Enable ADC
    while (!(ADC1_ISR & (1UL << 0))); // Wait for ADRDY
}

uint16_t adc_read(void) {
    ADC1_CR |= (1UL << 2); // Start conversion (ADSTART)
    while (!(ADC1_ISR & (1UL << 2))); // Wait for End-Of-Conversion (EOC)
    return (uint16_t)ADC1_DR;
}

int main(void) {
    hardware_init();
    while (1) {
        uint16_t raw = adc_read();
        // 45°C corresponds to raw threshold: (45.0 * 1023 / 100.0) ≈ 460
        if (raw >= 460) {
            // Hazard State: Green OFF, Red ON, Buzzer ON
            GPIOA_BSRR = (1UL << (10 + 16)); // PA10 LOW
            GPIOB_BSRR = (1UL << 3);         // PB3 HIGH
            GPIOB_BSRR = (1UL << 4);         // PB4 HIGH (Buzzer)
        } else {
            // Safe State: Green ON, Red OFF, Buzzer OFF
            GPIOA_BSRR = (1UL << 10);        // PA10 HIGH
            GPIOB_BSRR = (1UL << (3 + 16));  // PB3 LOW
            GPIOB_BSRR = (1UL << (4 + 16));  // PB4 LOW
        }
        for (volatile uint32_t i = 0; i < 600000; i++); // Short loop delay
    }
}

7. Hands-On Practice Challenge (For Students to Solve) 🎯

🎯 Objective & Scenario

In modern automotive systems, an Electronic Control Unit (ECU) streams periodic sensor telemetry packets over serial to a central dashboard. In this practice challenge, you will implement an Automotive Serial Diagnostic Console & Parameter Logger on the STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 that samples temperature on A0 and streams formatted ASCII telemetry frames over UART at 115200 baud, responding dynamically to interactive console commands.

🛠️ Hardware Setup (Wokwi)

The circuit connects to the STM32 Nucleo-C031C6 board: * 1x Potentiometer: Wiper to A0 (PA0), ends to GND and 3.3V. * Onboard LED: On D13 (PA5). * Virtual COM Port: USB Serial at 115200 baud.

8. Concept Check Practice Questions

Question 1: Serial vs. Parallel Bus Trade-Offs

NoteClick to View Question & Options

Why have modern high-speed embedded systems almost entirely shifted from parallel data buses to serial communication protocols?

Options:
- A) Parallel communication consumes more software memory in SRAM
- B) Clock skew and cross-talk between closely spaced parallel traces limit speed and distance, whereas serial lines eliminate inter-trace skew and require far fewer microcontroller pins
- C) Serial buses do not require a ground reference
- D) Parallel communication is only supported by 8-bit processors

Tip✅ Correct Answer: B

Explanation:
In parallel buses, signals on different wires travel at slightly different speeds due to physical trace length variations (clock skew). At high frequencies, this causes bits to arrive misaligned. Serial sends one bit at a time, eliminating skew while drastically cutting pin count and PCB complexity.

Question 2: \(I^2C\) Pull-Up Resistor Function

NoteClick to View Question & Options

In an \(I^2C\) communication circuit, what is the role of the external pull-up resistors on the SDA and SCL lines?

Options:
- A) To prevent the microcontroller from resetting during sleep mode
- B) Because \(I^2C\) devices have open-drain outputs that can only pull the lines LOW to \(0\text{V}\); pull-up resistors passively pull the lines back HIGH when released
- C) To step down \(5\text{V}\) signals to \(3.3\text{V}\)
- D) To generate the baud rate clock

Tip✅ Correct Answer: B

Explanation:
\(I^2C\) outputs are open-drain. A device can pull a line to ground, but cannot drive it high. When no device is asserting a 0, the external pull-up resistor pulls the voltage up to \(V_{DD}\), creating a wired-AND configuration that enables multi-master arbitration and clock stretching.
